Southpoint Residences

This is IGB's final parcel on its own 50-acre Mid Valley City masterplan — a sky-bridge connecting simultaneously to Mid Valley Megamall and The Gardens Mall, an integration no other residence in KL can match. That is the real floor under both the scarcity and connectivity scores. The trade-off is just as concrete: the title is a commercial-titled service residence (not residential title), and the representative unit at RM 1,700,000 (1,116 sqft) works out to a gross yield of roughly 5.1% — respectable for an ultra-luxury product, but the commercial title still drags on next-buyer financing and the exit score. This is Tan & Tan's first Build-Then-Sell project — completed in 2023, only released for sale in September 2024, and reportedly about 50% sold as of early 2026, mostly to local buyers — meaning the project has no resale (Brickz) history of its own yet, so confidence in the valuation and exit dimensions is honestly marked low.

◆ Exit Reality Check

Exit Reality Check

The v5.0 three-part check: scarcity audit (is there a supply-side moat), next-buyer pyramid (who buys it in 10 years), and secondary-market evidence (turnover / listing rate / discount). This project is a primary-sales, Build-Then-Sell case (launched 2024-09, with no resale record of its own yet); the secondary-market evidence uses same-complex Northpoint Residences (completed 2006, 228 units) as a proxy, with confidence marked low — same complex, but a large product-tier gap, so this is the closest available geographic proxy, not a same-tier anchor.

5A · Scarcity Audit (Supply-Side, Three Layers)
Can the market reproduce another one of these in 10 years? This project's land layer is capped, but the regulatory layer offers no lock-in tool, and the product layer is partially replicable.
✓Land layer: Southpoint Tower is the final parcel of IGB's 50-acre Mid Valley City masterplan; the developer has publicly stated there is no further development planned on the site once this project is complete — a settled fact, not marketing language.
△Product layer: Simultaneous sky-bridge access to both Mid Valley Megamall and The Gardens Mall has no second example in KL — that specific integration is irreplicable. But the jumbo unit configurations (many over 2,000 sqft) could in principle still be replicated by other luxury developments along the same corridor (e.g. future towers at SP Setia's Federal Hill).
✗Regulatory layer: Leasehold plus commercial title, with no freehold or height-cap mechanism providing a legal supply lock. Purely from a title and zoning standpoint, this project's scarcity comes from "facts that have already happened" (masterplan closure, dual-mall integration), not from a regulatory tool itself.
Effective-scarcity verdict: The land-layer and partial product-layer scarcity are real and verifiable (masterplan closure and dual-mall sky-bridge access are both checkable facts), but demand-side evidence remains thin — there is no Brickz resale premium of this project's own to cite. The only demand evidence available is primary-sales velocity: launched September 2024, roughly 50% sold as of early 2026, mostly to affluent local buyers. This proves real demand exists at the RM 1.7M+ price point, but it is primary-absorption evidence, not resale-premium evidence, so the scarcity adjustment takes only +0.4 (dual-mall integration) rather than stacking the full +1.0 available when all three layers pass cleanly.
5B · Next-Buyer Pyramid
Quantum test: Representative unit RM 1,700,000 → next-buyer household income threshold of roughly RM 18,700/month (price × 1.1%: 90% LTV, 4.3%, 30 years, 40% debt-service burden) → against KL's median household income of RM 10,805 (DOSM Household Income Survey 2024, the highest in the country), the threshold is roughly 1.73x the KL median, estimated to sit in roughly the top 15–20% of KL's income distribution (estimated; DOSM does not publish exact percentiles). This isn't institutional-only territory, but it clearly exceeds the comfort zone of a typical "urban upgrader" household, sitting closer to the affluent professional/business-owner band.

◆ In-Depth Analysis

Project Deep-Dive

Southpoint Residences sits on Lingkaran Syed Putra, the final parcel in IGB Berhad's 50-acre Mid Valley City masterplan, developed through its subsidiary Tan & Tan Developments Berhad. That masterplan broke ground in 1993 and made its public debut in 1999 with the opening of Mid Valley Megamall; today it has grown into a mature, self-owned ecosystem spanning two shopping malls, three hotels, seven office towers, and now residential towers. Southpoint occupies the upper floors of Menara Southpoint, a 59-storey mixed tower whose lower 27 floors are Grade A office space and whose upper 22 floors hold the project's 172 service-residence units, ranging from 1,116 to 5,837 sqft across 2- to 5-bedroom configurations, with the largest units getting their own private lift lobbies. IGB has publicly stated that once this project is complete, there will be no further development on the site — that isn't marketing copy; it is the actual source of the 6.6 score on the scarcity dimension.

The single most irreplicable thing about this project is that its sky-bridge connects simultaneously to Mid Valley Megamall (1.8 million sqft of net lettable area) and The Gardens Mall. No other residential project in KL walks straight into two malls of this caliber at once, and both are IGB-owned and operated through IGB REIT, sustaining occupancy above 99% for years — about as pure an example of landlord-operator alignment as this market offers, and the main reason the developer dimension lands at 8.3 (7.5 baseline + 0.5 for ecosystem alignment + 0.3 for Build-Then-Sell's zero-completion-risk). Connectivity scores 7.5, held back by one clear weak point: there is no direct MRT or LRT station here. The Mid Valley KTM Komuter station sits roughly 200 meters from the complex overall, but the actual walk to Menara Southpoint specifically takes 12 to 15 minutes, leaving a free shuttle bus to Bangsar LRT as the practical backup. The dual-mall integration already carries a weight that even some Tier 9 comparables in this framework don't have, but the rail access here is genuinely weaker than at KLCC or TRX.

There is one fact about this project that has to be disclosed plainly: Southpoint Residences is Tan & Tan's first Build-Then-Sell project. The residential component was completed in 2023, yet it wasn't formally offered for sale until September 2024. That means this project has no Brickz resale transaction history of its own to date — the valuation dimension (5.8) and the exit dimension (5.3 local / 4.0 foreign) both have to lean on same-complex Northpoint Residences (completed 2006, 228 units, a standard-title condominium) as a geographic proxy, and Northpoint is a fundamentally different product tier: applying its transaction median directly (RM 717 psf) would badly understate this project's true value. Cross-checking instead against Northpoint's current listing ceiling (RM 1,317 psf) against this project's representative RM 1,524 psf shows roughly a 15.7% premium, landing in the "elevated, needs a catalyst" bracket; checking again against a comparable cross-district luxury service residence, The Establishment @ Bangsar (listing ceiling RM 2,387 psf), this project instead looks inexpensive. The two signals point in opposite directions — which is exactly why this card marks the valuation confidence honestly as "low" rather than manufacturing a false sense of certainty.

On the cashflow side there is a finding worth correcting an initial assumption over: the representative 1,116 sqft unit rents at RM 7,200/month for a gross yield of 5.08%, which is actually decent for an ultra-luxury service residence and does not fall into the common trap where jumbo-unit products automatically carry weak yields. Local and foreign cashflow scores are 5.7 and 3.6 respectively, a 2.1-point gap that sits within the framework's allowed 1.5–2.5 range but is smaller than at most commercial SA projects on this site, simply because the underlying gross yield itself holds up reasonably well. The real exit question centers on the next-buyer pool: the quantum test puts the representative unit's income threshold at roughly RM 18,700/month, about 1.73x KL's median household income (RM 10,805), placing it in roughly the top 15–20% of KL's income distribution — not institutional-only territory, but well past the comfort zone of a typical urban upgrader household, sitting closer to affluent professionals or business owners. Foreign buyers are, somewhat counterintuitively, in a relatively stronger position at this price point: the representative unit clears the RM 1 million Federal Territory threshold by roughly 70%, a far more comfortable buffer than the many commercial SA projects that sit right at the edge of that line, and service residences are confirmed as MM2H-eligible property. But public reporting shows that, as of early 2026, primary sales have gone predominantly to affluent local buyers — a positive sign in that it means the exit isn't hostage to foreign-policy swings, but also a reminder that Mid Valley isn't yet the kind of location with decades of active foreign resale activity that Mont Kiara or Desa ParkCity can claim.

Two supply-side numbers are worth watching. NAPIC's latest release puts KL's broad service-apartment overhang at 4,181 units, with 58.5% of the national glut concentrated in the RM 500,000–1,000,000 band — this project's representative unit at RM 1.7 million already sits above that band, which is one reason the appreciation dimension scores 5.7, above the 5.0 commercial-SA baseline. The other is SP Setia's Federal Hill masterplan (52 acres, first phase Parkside Residences at 693 units) in the same "New Golden Triangle" / Bangsar–KL Sentral corridor, roughly 2 to 3 kilometers away — its current unit mix (485 to 1,325 sqft) targets a different segment than this project's jumbo units, but it proves the concept of a large integrated masterplan can be replicated in this same corridor, even if the specific scale and price band haven't lined up directly yet.

Taken together, Southpoint Residences' investment thesis rests on something that has already happened and cannot be undone: IGB used up the last parcel of Mid Valley City to build the only residential product in KL with simultaneous sky-bridge access to two of its own flagship malls. That floor is real, and so is the developer's track record and ecosystem alignment. But buyers need to be clear about exactly what they're buying: a completed, brand-new project with no checkable resale history, where commercial-title financing friction will materially tighten next-buyer terms, and where a representative-unit price above RM 1.7 million narrows the local buyer pool from "urban upgrader" down to "affluent professional." What makes this card honest isn't a flattering score — it's being explicit about which judgments are backed by an actual Brickz premium and which ones can only be inferred from primary-sales velocity and a geographic proxy. Once Southpoint develops its own secondary market, this card will need to be revisited.

10-Year Scenario Benchmarks: assuming a 2026 entry into an already-completed residential unit (representative unit RM 1,700,000):
  • Base case (3%/year price growth): IGB's ecosystem premium holds, primary sales complete as planned within 2026, and dual-mall integration sustains stable tenant demand. Malaysian buyer's 10-year cumulative net return is roughly RM 483,000, IRR roughly 4.1%; foreign buyer roughly RM 292,000, IRR roughly 2.1%.
  • Bull case (5%/year price growth): Mid Valley's masterplan-closure scarcity is fully realized, the dual-mall integration premium widens, and MM2H high-net-worth demand accelerates absorption. Malaysian buyer roughly RM 956,000, IRR roughly 8.6%; foreign buyer roughly RM 717,000, IRR roughly 5.9%.
  • Bear case (1%/year price growth): KL's broad service-apartment overhang keeps building, corridor competitors like Setia Federal Hill draw off demand, and primary sales fall short of a 2026 sellout. Malaysian buyer still nets a modest roughly +RM 85,000, IRR roughly 0.6%; foreign buyer loses roughly RM 65,000, IRR roughly −0.5%.
10-year exit liquidity: this project has no resale history of its own; using same-complex Northpoint as a proxy (annualized turnover ~5.3%, listing rate ~14.9%, though the product-tier gap is large), the Malaysian exit is estimated at 6–12 months from listing to sale; the foreign exit, constrained by this district's unproven foreign-resale demand depth, is estimated at 10–18 months. Annual price growth needed to break even on all costs plus a 5% opportunity cost: roughly 1.7%/year for Malaysian buyers, roughly 3.5%/year for foreign buyers.

Highlights & Things to Watch

✦ Highlights
  • The final parcel of the Mid Valley City masterplan — IGB has publicly stated there is no further development planned on the site; supply closure is a settled fact, not marketing language
  • Dual-mall sky-bridge access: Mid Valley Megamall (1.8M sqft NLA) + The Gardens Mall, with no second example anywhere in KL; both malls are IGB-owned and sustaining 99%+ occupancy
  • Developer score 8.3: IGB/Tan & Tan (Bursa Tier 1) + strong landlord-ecosystem alignment + Build-Then-Sell zero-completion-risk (buyers see the finished product, with none of the abandonment/delay risk of traditional off-plan sales)
  • 5.08% gross yield, better than most ultra-luxury service residences covered on this site, avoiding the common trap where jumbo-unit products carry weak yields
  • The foreign representative unit at RM 1,700,000 clears the RM 1M threshold by roughly 70%, a far more comfortable buffer than most commercial SA projects sitting at the threshold's edge; service residences are confirmed MM2H-eligible
  • Low–medium foreign dependency: primary sales have gone mostly to affluent local buyers, so the exit isn't hostage to a single foreign-policy swing
⚠ Things to Watch
  • This project has no Brickz resale history of its own: sales only launched September 2024, so the valuation and exit dimensions rely on cross-checks against same-complex Northpoint and a cross-district comparable, with confidence honestly marked low — buyers should get an independent valuation
  • Commercial-titled service residence (not residential title): −0.3 on Malaysian cashflow, and next-buyer financing friction (80–85% LTV, +0.5–1% rate) weighs on the exit score
  • High quantum threshold: the representative unit's income threshold is about 1.73x KL's median household income, narrowing the local buyer pool to affluent professionals/business owners rather than a typical urban upgrader
  • Mid Valley/KL Eco City has not yet shown the decades-deep active foreign resale community that Mont Kiara or Desa ParkCity have; foreign demand depth here currently rests on eligibility, not verified actual transactions
  • Some listing portals show "completed 2020," which conflicts with the cross-checked "completed 2023, sales launched September 2024" — buyers should confirm against the SPA and official developer documents before signing
  • Supply side: KL's broad service-apartment overhang is at 4,181 units (NAPIC Q1 2026); SP Setia's Federal Hill masterplan (52 acres) roughly 2–3km away is rolling out and could draw off affluent demand over time
  • The exact lease expiry has not been individually verified; using same-complex Northpoint's 2103 expiry as a reference implies roughly 77 years remaining — buyers should confirm this in writing with the developer before signing

Fit for this project: This project's generational-hold case rests on two genuinely solid pillars — first, the Mid Valley City masterplan has already closed, so this location's scarcity won't be diluted by fresh supply on the same site; second, IGB, as both landlord and operator, has a two-decade-plus track record of maintaining Mid Valley Megamall and The Gardens Mall, evidence it can sustain this ecosystem's asset quality over the long run. But two limitations deserve equally honest treatment: (1) leasehold plus commercial title is less clean than freehold residential title for inheritance timing and taxation, and next-generation heirs or resellers will still face financing friction; (2) this project has no long-hold or resale case history of its own at all — 20 years out, its building depreciation and management quality are harder to forecast than same-complex Northpoint, which has already been operating for nearly 20 years. Compared with freehold assets like Damansara Heights or Desa ParkCity where the land layer itself is scarce, this project's generational case leans on its ecosystem and brand rather than the title itself.
